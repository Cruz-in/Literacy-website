## 2. DEFINITION ⚠️ *your words*

**Heading:** WHAT IS IMAGERY

> Imagery is descriptive language that pulls the five senses into writing. Instead of just telling you what happened, it lets you see the light, hear the quiet, and feel the cold — so the scene plays out in your head like a memory you actually lived.

---

## 3. THE FIVE SENSES

**SIGHT**
> The last light breaks through the branches in thin gold lines, and the path ahead fades slowly into blue.

**SOUND**
> Somewhere behind you a branch settles. Everything else has gone quiet enough to hear your own breathing.

**SMELL**
> Wet earth and pine hang in the cooling air, sharp and clean after the day's heat.

**TOUCH**
> The cold creeps in first at your fingertips, and the rough bark bites back when you reach out to steady yourself.

**TASTE**
> The air itself tastes faintly of rain, metallic and cool on the tip of your tongue.

## 5. REAL-WORLD EXAMPLE ✅ *keep the citation exact*

**Heading:** REAL-WORLD EXAMPLE — how a poet does it

> "The only other sound's the sweep / Of easy wind and downy flake."

**Citation:** — Robert Frost, *Stopping by Woods on a Snowy Evening* (1923)

**Analysis:**
> Frost leans on sound and touch — the near-silence of the woods and the softness of falling snow — so you don't just picture the scene, you feel how still it is.

## SEPARATE DOCUMENT — EXPLANATION ⚠️ *your words · 8 marks · attach separately, NOT on the site*

> Imagery matters because it turns reading into experiencing. When a writer gives the brain real sensations — the cold on your fingertips, the smell of rain — the reader stops decoding words and starts living the scene. We remember what we feel far longer than what we're simply told, so imagery is what makes writing emotional and memorable. By pulling in all five senses, it builds empathy and lets a reader step into a moment that was never theirs.
